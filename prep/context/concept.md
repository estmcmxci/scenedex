What is a Factory Records–Style Catalog System?

A Factory Records–style catalog system is a creative, open-ended framework for publishing, indexing, and worldbuilding through a release catalog. It treats everything—from traditional media to ephemeral moments—as a potential “release” that contributes to the evolving identity of a label, collective, or artist.

This concept is inspired by the catalog practices of Factory Records, a Manchester-based record label active in the 1980s–1990s. Below are the five core principles that define this model:

⸻

1. 🎭 Anything Can Be a Release

Factory gave release numbers not only to albums and singles, but also to:
	•	Nightclubs (FAC 51 – The Haçienda)
	•	Posters and T-shirts (FAC 13T)
	•	Parties and events
	•	A house cat (FAC 191)
	•	A lawsuit (FAC 61)
	•	A founder’s coffin (FAC 501)

In this system, a release doesn’t need to conform to a format, production value, or even seriousness. It’s a symbolic gesture, a way of adding a story node to the ongoing mythos of the label.

⸻

2. 🗃️ Catalogs Are Bespoke Classification Systems

Factory invented their own internal cataloging logic:
	•	Prefixes like FACT (albums) and FAC (miscellaneous)
	•	Non-chronological numbering
	•	Gaps and repetitions by design

This allowed the catalog to become a narrative device in its own right, revealing relationships, intent, and humor in how things were organized—not just what was released.

⸻

3. 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Releases Can Be Created by the Community

Factory gave release numbers to:
	•	Fan-made zines (FAC 239)
	•	Albums released on other labels (FACT 500)

This blurred the lines between authorship and curation, positioning the catalog as a cultural ledger more than a rigid IP registry. Anyone could contribute meaningfully.

⸻

4. 🌐 Releases Are a Form of Worldbuilding

The Factory catalog forms a cohesive mythos. Together, the records, club, posters, lawsuits, and film (24 Hour Party People) tell the story of a subculture that continues to resonate decades later. The catalog becomes an intentional, accretive world—not just a database.

⸻

5. 🏷️ Labels Can Be Anything

Factory expanded the definition of a “record label” into:
	•	A cultural publishing platform
	•	A social architecture
	•	A vessel for lore

This redefinition influenced modern “metalabel” thinking, showing how artists and collectives can shape identity through release logic, not just content.

⸻

💡 TL;DR

A Factory-style catalog system:
	•	Accepts anything as a valid release (idea, object, event)
	•	Uses a custom classification scheme
	•	Allows community contributions and curation
	•	Builds narrative cohesion over time
	•	Reimagines the label as cultural OS

This is the conceptual foundation for an onchain catalog: a programmable system where anyone granted access can mint releases that add to a shared mythos—tracked immutably onchain, indexed via subnames (e.g. pda-001.palaupalau.eth), and surfaced on a decentralized website.
